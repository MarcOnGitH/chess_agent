PROMPT 1
What techniques can you use to improve on a minimax algorithm with alpha-beta pruning for chess?



ANSWER 1
There are several ways to make a chess engine significantly stronger than a basic Minimax + Alpha-Beta pruning implementation. Some improve search efficiency, while others improve the quality of the decisions themselves.

If you're implementing your own chess engine in C++ or Python, I'd approach the improvements in the following order.
1. Search algorithm improvements
These are generally the most important improvements because they allow the engine to search deeper without necessarily requiring more computational power.
1. Iterative deepening
Instead of searching directly to a fixed depth, search progressively deeper:
- Search depth 1.
- Search depth 2.
- Search depth 3.
- Continue until reaching your time or depth limit.
For example:
for depth in range(1, max_depth + 1):    best_move = minimax(position, depth)



Why it helps:
- You always have a valid best move available.
- Previous searches provide information that helps subsequent searches.
- It works particularly well with move ordering.
- It allows you to implement time-limited searches.
2. Move ordering
Alpha-Beta pruning becomes substantially more effective when promising moves are evaluated first.

For example, instead of examining moves in arbitrary order, prioritise:
1. Previous iteration's best move.
2. Transposition table's best move.
3. Captures of valuable pieces.
4. Checks.
5. Killer moves.
6. Other moves ordered using history scores.
Consider two possible move orders:
Poor move ordering
A
B
C
D
E

Promising moves might be examined last, reducing pruning.

Improved move ordering
D
B
E
A
C

Strong candidate moves are examined first, increasing pruning opportunities.

This can make an enormous difference to Alpha-Beta performance.
3. Principal Variation Search (PVS)
PVS is an optimisation of Alpha-Beta pruning.

The idea is that you assume your first move is the strongest move.
- Search the first move using a full alpha-beta window.
- Search subsequent moves using a narrow window.
- If a subsequent move proves better, research it using a full window.
This avoids spending unnecessary time fully evaluating moves that are unlikely to be better than your current best move.
4. Transposition tables
A transposition occurs when different move sequences lead to the same chess position.

For example:
- Move sequence A: Nf3, Nf6, Nc3.
- Move sequence B: Nc3, Nc6, Nf3.
Depending on the exact moves, these can reach equivalent positions.

Without a transposition table, your engine might evaluate the same position multiple times.

You can store previously calculated results in a hash table:
transposition_table[position_hash] = {    "depth": depth,    "score": score,    "best_move": best_move,    "flag": "EXACT"}



The flags generally represent:
- EXACT: The position's value was calculated exactly.
- LOWERBOUND: The actual value is at least this score.
- UPPERBOUND: The actual value is at most this score.
Use Zobrist hashing to generate efficient position hashes, accounting for piece placement, side to move, castling rights and en passant availability.

This is one of the most valuable additions to a chess engine.

2. Search depth improvements
Simply increasing search depth isn't always the best approach. You can selectively search certain positions more deeply.
5. Quiescence search
One major weakness of basic minimax is the horizon effect.

Imagine your engine searches to depth 4 and sees:
- A queen being captured immediately after its search horizon.
- A favourable material position before that capture.
It might incorrectly evaluate the position as favourable because it hasn't searched far enough to see the capture.

Quiescence search addresses this.

Instead of stopping at the nominal search depth, continue searching tactical moves until the position becomes relatively quiet.

Typically, you search:
- Captures.
- Promotions.
- Sometimes checks, depending on the implementation.
This prevents the engine from evaluating positions in the middle of tactical exchanges.
6. Null-move pruning
The idea is to temporarily give the opponent an extra turn by passing your move.

If your position is still sufficiently strong after allowing the opponent an additional move, you can often conclude that searching every possible move is unnecessary.

This can dramatically reduce the search tree.

However, it requires care:
- Avoid applying it when in check.
- Be careful in zugzwang positions.
- Consider disabling it in certain endgames.
7. Late Move Reductions (LMR)
Not all moves deserve equal search depth.

Suppose your engine has already examined several promising moves. A quiet move ranked 20th in move ordering is statistically less likely to be the best move.

LMR searches later moves at a reduced depth initially.

If a reduced-depth search suggests the move is promising, it gets searched again at full depth.

This can substantially increase search efficiency.
8. Futility pruning
If the maximum possible improvement from a move is unlikely to exceed the current alpha-beta threshold, you can skip searching it.

For example, if:
- Your current evaluation is already significantly below alpha.
- The move is quiet.
- The maximum plausible material or positional improvement is insufficient.
You may safely avoid spending time on that branch under appropriately conservative conditions.

3. Evaluation function improvements
Search algorithms determine how many positions your engine examines. The evaluation function determines how intelligently it judges those positions.

This is where you can improve the engine's actual chess understanding.
9. Improve material evaluation
Start with conventional material values:
Piece	Value
Pawn	100
Knight	320
Bishop	330
Rook	500
Queen	900
King	20,000*

The king's value is primarily used to prevent illegal king captures, not as a conventional evaluation value.

However, material alone is insufficient.

You should also consider:
- Bishop pair bonuses.
- Doubled pawns.
- Isolated pawns.
- Passed pawns.
- Rook placement.
- Pawn structure.
10. Piece-square tables (PST)
A piece-square table assigns different values to different squares depending on the piece.

For example:
- Knights generally benefit from central squares.
- Bishops benefit from open diagonals.
- Rooks benefit from open files.
- Pawns benefit from advancing appropriately.
- Kings generally prefer safety in the middlegame but activity in the endgame.








metalphaeton-chess.blog.jp


You can implement this using a table for each piece:
evaluation += piece_square_table[piece][square]



You can also have separate middlegame and endgame tables.
11. King safety
King safety is particularly important because material evaluation alone might consider a position favourable despite a dangerous attack.

Consider:
- Pawn shield around the king.
- Open files near the king.
- Enemy pieces attacking the king.
- Number of attackers.
- Weak squares around the king.
- Castling availability.
12. Mobility
Mobility measures how many useful legal moves your pieces have.

For example:
- A knight with several active squares is generally better than one trapped in a corner.
- Bishops benefit from unobstructed diagonals.
- Rooks benefit from open files.
You can introduce a mobility bonus into your evaluation function.
13. Tapered evaluation
This is a particularly useful technique.

Instead of using exactly the same evaluation weights throughout the game, interpolate between middlegame and endgame evaluation.

For example:
\[
Eval = \frac{MG \times Phase + EG \times (24-Phase)}{24}
\]

Where:
- MG = middlegame evaluation.
- EG = endgame evaluation.
- Phase = game phase, calculated from remaining material.
This allows your engine to adapt its positional priorities as pieces disappear.

4. More advanced techniques
Once you've implemented the previous improvements, there are several additional techniques worth exploring.
Technique	Purpose
Killer heuristic	Prioritises quiet moves that previously caused cutoffs
History heuristic	Learns which moves tend to perform well
Aspiration windows	Searches around the previous iteration's evaluation
Check extensions	Searches certain checking sequences more deeply
Singular extensions	Extends uniquely strong moves
Internal iterative deepening	Finds useful move-ordering information when unavailable
Bitboards	Makes board operations and move generation faster
Magic bitboards	Accelerates sliding-piece attack generation
Incremental evaluation	Avoids recalculating the entire evaluation after every move

An especially interesting one is aspiration windows.

If your previous iteration evaluated a position at +150 centipawns, you can initially search using a narrower window around that score rather than the full range.

If the score falls outside the window, widen it and search again.

This can improve alpha-beta efficiency.

5. What should you actually implement?
If I were developing a chess engine from scratch, I'd prioritise the following:
Recommended implementation roadmap
1. Foundation
   - Minimax
   - Alpha-Beta pruning
   - Basic material evaluation
   - Legal move generation
2. Search optimisation
   - Iterative deepening
   - Move ordering
   - Transposition tables
   - Zobrist hashing
3. Tactical improvements
   - Quiescence search
   - Killer heuristic
   - History heuristic
   - Principal Variation Search
4. Advanced pruning
   - Null-move pruning
   - Late Move Reductions
   - Futility pruning
   - Aspiration windows
5. Evaluation improvements
   - Piece-square tables
   - Pawn structure
   - King safety
   - Mobility
   - Tapered evaluation
6. Performance
   - Bitboards
   - Incremental evaluation
   - Efficient move generation
   - Search profiling

6. How much difference can these make?
The biggest advantage comes from combining these techniques rather than implementing them independently.

For example, iterative deepening supplies a previous best move, move ordering examines that move first, alpha-beta pruning eliminates branches, and transposition tables avoid repeating work.

These improvements reinforce each other.

A well-optimised traditional engine can become remarkably strong without machine learning or neural networks.

For reference, engines such as Stockfish use sophisticated combinations of search optimisations, evaluation techniques and neural-network-based evaluation.

One question about your project
Are you developing this chess engine as part of your Computer Science studies, or as a personal project?

I'm asking because there are two quite different directions:
1. Traditional chess engine: Optimise minimax, alpha-beta, move ordering, pruning and handcrafted evaluation.
2. Advanced chess engine: Explore NNUE evaluation, Monte Carlo methods, neural networks and techniques used in modern competitive engines.
I'd recommend mastering the first approach before moving towards the second.

The important distinction is that each piece has its own positional value for every square on the board.

Let's build on your understanding.

Illustrative heatmap, not a calibrated chess-engine PST.
You'd have an entirely different distribution of values for bishops, rooks, queens, etc.

For instance:
- Knights generally receive bonuses for occupying central squares.
- Bishops receive bonuses for occupying squares with good diagonal access.
- Rooks receive bonuses for occupying open files and the seventh rank.
- Kings receive different positional preferences depending on the game phase.
One important detail: White and Black can usually share the same table through vertical mirroring.
2. What happens when you actually evaluate a position?
This is where things become interesting.

Suppose your board contains:
- One white knight on f3.
- One white bishop on c4.
- One black knight on c6.
Your evaluation function identifies each piece and its current square.

It then performs three lookups:
1. Find the knight's positional value at f3 using knight_view.
2. Find the bishop's positional value at c4 using bishop_view.
3. Find the knight's positional value at c6 using knight_view, but mirror the coordinates because it's Black.
You add those values together, taking the perspective of the side you're evaluating.

Conceptually:
\[
PST_{\text{total}} =
\sum_{\text{pieces}} PST(\text{piece},\text{square})
\]

You combine this with your material evaluation:
\[
Evaluation = Material + PST_{\text{total}}
\]

For example, if a knight is worth 320 centipawns and its positional bonus is +35:
\[
KnightValue = 320+35=355
\]

This means two positions with identical material can receive different evaluations because their pieces occupy different squares.
3. Where do the numbers actually come from?
This is probably the most interesting part.

You have three main approaches:

A. Manually designed values

You assign values based on chess principles.

For example, you know knights generally benefit from centralisation, so you give central squares higher values.

This is excellent for understanding how evaluation works.

B. Established PSTs

You can study existing chess engines and published PSTs to understand how experienced engine developers assign positional values.

These give you a useful starting point.

C. Automated tuning

Eventually, you can develop a system that adjusts the values automatically based on game results.

For example:
- Your engine plays 10,000 games.
- You identify which PST configurations produce better results.
- You adjust the values.
- You repeat the process.
This is an introduction to evaluation-function optimisation.
4. There's one more important improvement: Game phases
You shouldn't necessarily use the same PST throughout an entire game.

Consider the king.

During the middlegame, you generally want your king protected and relatively sheltered.

During the endgame, you generally want your king to become an active piece, potentially moving towards the centre.

Consequently, you can maintain two sets of PSTs:
Piece	Middlegame PST	Endgame PST
Pawn	Yes	Yes
Knight	Yes	Yes
Bishop	Yes	Yes
Rook	Yes	Yes
Queen	Yes	Yes
King	Yes	Yes

You then calculate a game-phase value based on the remaining material and interpolate between the two evaluations.

This is called tapered evaluation.

5. One conceptual distinction I'd encourage you to understand
PSTs evaluate where a piece is, not necessarily how good its current position actually is.

For example, a knight might occupy an excellent central square according to its PST, but that square might be controlled by an enemy pawn and the knight could be trapped.

Your PST doesn't inherently understand that.

That's why PSTs work best alongside other evaluation features, such as:
- Mobility.
- Pawn structure.
- King safety.
- Piece activity.
- Outposts.
- Threats and attacks.
My recommendation: Start with the six piece-specific tables, implement mirrored lookup for Black, and incorporate their values into your existing evaluation function.

Once that's working, experiment with middlegame/endgame PSTs. That will give you a very good understanding of how positional evaluation develops beyond simple material counting.

ANSWER 3
Personally, yes. I'd implement iterative deepening before PSTs, but there's an important distinction: iterative deepening improves your search, whereas PSTs improve your evaluation. They're largely independent improvements.

I'd actually approach your project in stages rather than trying to implement everything at once.
1. Iterative deepening vs PSTs
Consider what each one contributes:
Feature	What it improves
Iterative deepening	Search strategy
PSTs	Positional understanding
Transposition tables	Search efficiency
Move ordering	Alpha-beta pruning efficiency
Quiescence search	Tactical evaluation

My reasoning for prioritising iterative deepening:
- It's relatively straightforward to understand.
- It provides a foundation for other search optimisations.
- It makes move ordering more effective.
- It gives you a natural way to implement time-controlled searches.
However, PSTs are also relatively straightforward and would be a perfectly reasonable next implementation if you're particularly interested in evaluation.
2. About Zobrist hashing
The algorithm you're thinking of is Zobrist hashing.

And honestly, don't let the name intimidate you. The underlying concept is considerably simpler than it initially appears.

The purpose is to assign a unique-ish numerical identifier to a chess position.

Imagine you have:
- 12 possible piece types (six white, six black).
- 64 possible board squares.
- Two possible sides to move.
- Four possible castling rights.
- Eight possible en passant files.
You generate random 64-bit integers for these different characteristics.

For example, conceptually:
- White knight on f3 → random number A.
- White pawn on e4 → random number B.
- Black king on e8 → random number C.
- White to move → random number D.
You XOR these numbers together to produce the position's hash.
\[
Hash = A \oplus B \oplus C \oplus D \oplus \dots
\]

The important property is that XOR is reversible:
\[
A \oplus B \oplus B = A
\]

This means you can efficiently update the hash when pieces move rather than recalculating the entire position from scratch.

You also account for castling rights and en passant availability, because these affect which moves are legal.
The basic idea
Chess position

Random numbers corresponding to board characteristics

XOR combination

64-bit position hash


You can then use that hash as a key in your transposition table.

The table stores previously calculated evaluations, search depths, bounds and best moves.

You don't need to understand every mathematical detail of hashing to implement it successfully.
